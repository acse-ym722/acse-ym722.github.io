---
title: How Ordinary People Can Regain Agency in the Age of AI
date: 2026-11-03
category: Personal essay
kind: essay
excerpt: AI brings efficiency, anxiety, and wider access to powerful tools. Perhaps it can also help ordinary people realize long-shelved ideas and become creators of change.
---

Lately, I have often felt two emotions at once: excitement about AI and unease about the future.

These feelings did not come out of nowhere. Large language models now do much more than chat. They operate software, handle office tasks, create presentations automatically, and help people write and code. Work that once took half a day can now produce an initial result in very little time. Greater efficiency is welcome, of course, but another question quickly follows: if the amount of work stays the same while everyone works faster, what happens to the people who are no longer needed?

Not everyone is becoming more efficient at the same pace, either. Some people are already proficient with these tools, while others do not know where to begin. The gaps created by technology do not always close on their own; sometimes they first become even more visible.

I did not experience the internet boom around 2000, so I can only look back at that period. People then probably felt a similar sense of helplessness. The dot-com bubble was real, and some of the dire warnings may have had merit. Yet the ubiquitous connectivity that once sounded like fantasy has become an ordinary part of life. When a new technology emerges, it is difficult to tell what is a bubble and what will endure. In hindsight, everything seems to follow a discernible path.

This wave of AI happens to coincide with an economic downturn. People's circumstances are harder, and their anxiety is heavier. Sometimes I reassure myself that AI has at least brought a kind of technological leveling. Things I once could not learn or sustain can now be explained at any time. Steps that once required a professional can now be attempted on my own.

But on further reflection, leveling may only mean putting tools into more hands, not bringing everyone to the same destination. People who cannot code can now build software, but what to build, how to build it, and how far to take it still depend on judgment, taste, and an instinct for opportunity. Some people really are better at finding solutions and identifying what matters amid uncertainty.

I once heard that even if the national college entrance examination were replaced with esports, many of those who would have entered Tsinghua or Peking University might still rise to the top. The claim is too absolute, but I understand its point. What truly sets people apart may not be a particular skill, but the ability to understand rules, train consistently, identify problems, and adjust. These capacities may remain valuable even when the environment changes.

For a while, this line of thinking made me pessimistic. Then I recently came across OPC, the idea of a one-person company, and realized that the same development can feel very different from another position.

From an employee's perspective, a one-person company may suggest that employers can finally use AI to do everything themselves, with layoffs naturally following. But from another angle, could ordinary people also become their own bosses? One person may now be able to prototype what once required a team. Collaboration inherently loses information; an idea often changes after being relayed several times. If one person can carry a project from design through execution, small, specific ideas may become easier to realize.

Of course, not everyone is suited to starting a company, and a one-person company is by no means easy. But at least there seems to be one more path.

This feels concrete to me. I have long enjoyed RC cars, and I occasionally imagine designs of my own. No manufacturer would develop a product around an ordinary hobbyist's idea, so in the past I could only imagine. Mechanics, control systems, and modeling each take considerable time to learn, making it easy to set the hobby aside.

Now it seems possible to try. I can ask AI to guide me through unfamiliar topics, help analyze designs, and learn modeling and coding while working. Finally, I can use 3D printing to turn the design into physical parts. The first result may be crude or even unusable, but an idea in my mind can at least become something real in my hands.

This was the first time I truly understood, in concrete terms, that AI does not only mean employers can hire fewer people. It can also mean that an ordinary person finally has the chance to make something they have carried in their mind for years.

It also helps me better understand why AI development is being promoted so strongly. An increase in one person's productive capacity may seem like an individual change, but when it happens to many people, society's productive capacity changes as well. I cannot say with confidence how long the painful transition will last. Macro-level optimism cannot erase the distress of a particular person losing a job. As technology advances, some people are left behind, and that cannot be dismissed with a simple reference to historical patterns.

Still, I do not want to see only layoffs and replacement. Once efficiency improves, can people gain time to do other things? Can ideas once constrained by knowledge, cost, and the conditions for collaboration now be attempted? These questions deserve serious thought as well.

In the age of AI, perhaps what ordinary people can do is keep learning throughout life: continue exploring new tools, follow other people's progress, listen to different ideas, and refine our thinking through encounters with others. We do not need to compete with AI on speed alone. We should practice using it to ask better questions and make things we could not make before.

The future remains uncertain. But at least we can be more than people waiting for change to happen; we can also become people who create change with our own hands.
